Contact (1997) is really about belief. It's not very subtle about it, but I think it's still worth to stop and appreciate how beautifully it layers its questions of belief, how it makes you ask them yourself all throughout it, and how self-referential it all ends up being.

## polarities
Throughout the film, there are polarities of belief.
- Drumlin cuts off Ellie's research because he deems it a "waste of time", when she (all while not having anything to show for it) whole heartedly believes in it.
- For years she goes from investor to investor, trying to get someone to fund her research (and effectively -) to believe in it (her), everyone (until Hadden) declines. 

Between Drumlin and Ellie:

> Drumlin: "I know you must think this is all very unfair. Maybe that's an understatement. What you don't know is I agree. I wish the world was a place where fair was the bottom line, where the kind of idealism you showed at the hearing was rewarded, not taken advantage of. Unfortunately, we don't live in that world."  
Ellie: "Funny, I've always believed that the world is what we make of it".

When asked by Palmer in a hearing if she believes in god, she says:
> "As a scientist I rely on empirical evidence, in this matter, I don't believe there is data either way."

She cannot get herself to say yes, even though it's clearly the answer the selection team expects (and indeed she gets rejected for the position for it).

Drumlin on the other hand just says exactly what they want to hear and gets the mission. 

-> Drumlin is a realist and opportunist, while Ellie is an idealist.

-> In a similar manner we see Palmer as spiritual, and Ellie as grounded in truth and empiricism.


By embedding different polarities of belief into the characters actions, the film makes you internally debate the same questions. For example right after the selection scene (but no later) you'd think maybe Ellie should've been an opportunist like Drumlin and said what the selection team wanted to hear.


## boomerangs

Another repeating theme is Ellie's arguments being used against her.
- In Palmer and Ellie's conversation on god she says:
> "How do you know you're not deluding yourself?  
I'd need proof"

She is then asked by Palmer:

> Palmer: Did you love your father?  
Ellie: What?  
Palmer: Your dad. Did you love him?  
Ellie: Yes, very much.  
Palmer: Prove it.


- In the same conversation she gives Occam's razor as an argument against god:

> "What's more likely, an all powerful mysterious god created the universe and then decided to not give any proof of his existence, OR that he simply doesn't exist at all and that we created him so that we wouldn't have to feel so small and alone?". 

Later in the film when confronted by Kitz on her experience on Vega, he again presents Occam's razor and says:
> "What is more likely here, that a message from aliens results in a magical machine that whisks you away to the center of the galaxy to go windsurfing with dear old dad and then a split second later returns you home without a single shred of proof, OR that your experience is the result of being the unwitting star in the farewell performance of one S.R. Hadden, the man with the means the motive and the opportunity to play you and indeed the rest of us as pawns in the biggest most elaborate the most expensive hoax of all time." 

- Actually, you may notice in the same final hearing, the whole line of questioning by Kitz is done by the kind of empirical analysis Ellie always rooted for.


The point of the boomerangs is to make you question your own arguments and see how they could be used on the opposite view.

## rebirth
The whole scene of the bridge to the machine is embedded with themes of death.
Mikehill did a really good video on the leap of faith theme here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HYdSKQyff-8

In short;
- The plank-like bridge to the machine symbolizes execution by drowning.
- The shot to the bottom associates circling sharks.
- The chair she needs to plug into resembles an electrifying chair.
- The guards costumes are akin to bomb-shelter wardens' suits.

(And more, highly recommend to watch the vid).

The ascension, I think, is strictly present in her words during the travel to Vega:
- "Some kind of an electromagnetic field" - trying to hold on to presuppositions.
- "I can't describe it, I can't even explain it" - the bridge between her (attempts of) objective analysis of reality and her subjective experience is beginning to collapse.
- "I see a light" - her entire perception has now effectively altered (also emphasizes the overwhelming death motif of the scene, as a near-death-experience that is often described).
- "Oh God!" - said after almost 2 hours of the film where we're constantly reminded of her stance on god.
- "They should've sent a poet" - a final remark of transformation and acknowledgment by Ellie of the value of (as Palmer put it) spirituality.

And of course eventually she arrives at the Pensacola-like beach she drew when she was little, "finalizing" the rebirth.

The point of metaphorical rebirth in stories, I think, is to transform your perception too, here it's to look at the questions of the film from a belief standpoint.


### irony

The irony in the film is that Ellie, although self-claimed the opposite, is more of a "believer" than anyone. Her belief system is different but is stronger than the bureaucrats, Drumlin and perhaps even Palmer. The only reason she got so far is because she actually believed in the SETI.

In rebirth, she becomes conscious of it,
> “Rebirth is not a process that we can in any way observe. We can neither measure nor weigh nor photograph it. It is entirely beyond sense perception… One speaks of rebirth; one professes rebirth; one is filled with rebirth… We have to be content with its psychic reality.” - Jung in "Concerning Rebirth"

## self-reference
Even with all the clues at the end it still isn't conclusive whether or not she has actually been to Vega or hallucinated it.  
And of course at the end of the film Ellie admits she cannot prove it, that they just have to believe it;
coming back full circle while also exemplifying her transformation.

The interesting thing about the film is that it's self-referential. You're forced into the question of belief the characters themselves face.
